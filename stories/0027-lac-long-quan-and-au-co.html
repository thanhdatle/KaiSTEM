🐉 Lạc Long Quân and Âu Cơ

A long picture book to flip through together — the Vietnamese legend of a dragon from the sea, a fairy from the mountains, and the hundred children who hatched from one bundle of eggs. Our family's own story about where we come from.

~16 min, in six short parts (stop at any part)· Kai's jobs: call "Bố ơi!", roar, drum, count to seven, crack the eggs, say "Con Rồng cháu Tiên!"

A legend from Vietnam

Lạc Long Quân and Âu Cơ

"Story, story — are your ears open?" Kai: "Open!"

Part 1 · The dragon of the sea

Long, long ago, deep, deep under the sea, there was a shining water palace.

In it lived Lạc Long Quân, a dragon lord. His name has the word Long in it, an old word for dragon.

His father was Kinh Dương Vương, a king of the land. His mother was a dragon princess from a great lake.

So Lạc Long Quân was part dragon. He could swim like a fish — swish, swish! Kai's hand swims — and be a great dragon whenever he liked.

But sometimes he took the shape of a man and walked up out of the waves to visit the people of the land.

"Hello, everyone!" Kai waves

He loved the people, and the people loved him.

"If you ever need help," he said, "just call me: Bố ơi!" Bố means Dad. That's what the people called him, as if he were their own father. Kai: "Bố ơi!"

Then — splash! Kai claps — back into the sea he went, home to his water palace.

Part 2 · "Bố ơi, come and help us!"

Out in the sea lived a giant fish spirit, Ngư Tinh. It thrashed its tail and made huge waves, and the little fishing boats rocked and wobbled.

Nobody dared to go out fishing.

So the people stood on the shore and called: "Bố ơi, Bố ơi! Come and help us!" Kai calls

And whoosh! — up from the sea came Lạc Long Quân.

He sailed out in a long boat. The giant fish opened its big mouth wide — so Lạc Long Quân tossed in a hot, glowing stone!

"Hot, hot, hot!" Kai fans his mouth

"Ptooey!" The fish spat it out and swam away, far, far away, and it never bothered the boats again.

Out sailed the fishing boats, happy as can be.

On the land lived a tricky fox spirit, Hồ Tinh, with nine bushy tails. Kai counts the tails

It hid in a cave in a rocky hill, and it played mean tricks on the village and snatched people's things.

"Bố ơi, Bố ơi! Come and help us!" Kai calls Whoosh! There was Lạc Long Quân.

He called up the water of the sea, and a giant wave came rolling in — swoosh!

The water washed the rocky hill away, and the fox ran off, tails flying, far into the forest.

Where the hill had been, a big lake filled up. People say it's Hồ Tây, the West Lake in Hà Nội.

Up in the hills stood a huge old tree, so old that it had turned into a grumpy tree spirit, Mộc Tinh.

It stomped its roots and shook its branches — stomp, stomp, shake! Kai stomps — and everybody hid.

"Bố ơi, Bố ơi! Come and help us!" Kai calls Whoosh!

Lạc Long Quân came as a great dragon, and he gave the biggest dragon roar: "RRROAR!" Kai roars

The tree spirit got such a fright that it hurried away over the mountains — stomp, stomp, stomp — and it never came back.

The white egrets flew home to the trees, and everyone came out smiling.

Then Lạc Long Quân taught the people many things.

He showed them how to plant rice in the wet, muddy fields: push it in, one little plant at a time. Kai pushes a finger down — "Plant!"

He showed them how to cook rice in green bamboo tubes over the fire.

"Mmm — warm rice!" Kai rubs his tummy

And the people built their houses up high on tall posts, safe and dry from floods and from wild animals.

Up the ladder — climb, climb, climb! Kai's hands climb

Part 3 · The fairy of the mountains

High, high in the mountains lived a fairy, a Tiên, named Âu Cơ.

She loved flowers, and birds, and the cool green hills.

One day Âu Cơ heard that the land of the Lạc people, far below, had beautiful flowers and plants found nowhere else.

"I want to see them!" she said. And down the mountain path she went.

Lạc Long Quân was by the river when he saw her among the flowers.

"Hello!" he said. "Hello!" said Âu Cơ. And they both smiled. Kai: "Hello!"

They talked and talked: about the deep blue sea and the tall green mountains, about fish and flowers, dragons and birds.

They liked each other very much.

So Lạc Long Quân and Âu Cơ got married, and they lived together on the land.

Everyone came with drums and music: "Tùng, tùng, tùng!" Kai drums

Part 4 · One hundred eggs

Then Âu Cơ had a very big surprise. It wasn't one baby. It was a bọc: a big, soft bundle, like a silky sack.

"Oh!" said Lạc Long Quân. Kai: "Oh!"

They waited, and waited. One day, two days…

Kai counts One, two, three, four, five, six, seven days!

On the seventh day, the bọc opened, and inside were eggs. Lots and lots of eggs!

How many? Kai guesses One hundred eggs!

Crack! Crack! Crack! Kai claps

Out of every egg came a baby. One hundred babies!

The hundred children grew up fast and strong. The old story says they didn't even need milk! And they were all clever and brave.

Can you count ten of them? Kai counts

A hundred children means a hundred breakfasts, a hundred naps, and a hundred big hugs every day!

Part 5 · The mountain and the sea

But Lạc Long Quân was a dragon, and he missed the sea.

He missed the cool, deep water, and the fish, and his shining water palace.

He went home to the sea, and he stayed, and stayed. Âu Cơ and the children missed him, so they went to the shore and called:

"Bố ơi, Bố ơi! Come back to us!" Kai calls Whoosh! And there he was.

"Let's all stay together," said Âu Cơ.

Lạc Long Quân said gently, "I am a dragon, from the deep water. You are a fairy, from the high mountains. Water and mountains are very different."

"The sea calls me home, and the mountains call you. So let's share. Fifty children will come with me to the sea, and fifty will go with you to the mountains."

Fifty and fifty make one hundred.

"And we will always help each other. Whenever you need us, just call, and we will come."

And everybody said: "Mountain or sea — we'll help each other!" Kai says it too

So they had a big family hug, and said goodbye.

Fifty children went down to the sea with their father, and fifty went up into the mountains with their mother, waving and waving. Kai waves They were still one family.

And whenever they missed each other, they called — and they came.

Part 6 · Children of the Dragon

In the mountains, the eldest son grew up to be the very first king: Hùng Vương, the Hùng King.

He called his land Văn Lang.

All those children came from one bọc. And Vietnamese people call each other đồng bào, "born of the same bundle", like brothers and sisters.

And that's why they say they are con Rồng cháu Tiên: children of the Dragon, grandchildren of the Fairy.

"Dragon from the sea!" Kai: "Fairy from the mountains!"

And you, Kai? Your family comes from Việt Nam too.

So you are con Rồng cháu Tiên as well — a little bit dragon, a little bit fairy! "Con Rồng cháu Tiên!" Kai says it

The End

"Snip, snap, snout — this tale's told out."

Driving? Tell it without the page · six beats

The picture book is for home, bedtime, or a passenger. In the car, tell it from memory — bold is Kai's part. It also splits neatly: beats 1–3 one day, 4–6 the next.

  1. Long ago, Lạc Long Quân, a dragon lord, lived in a water palace under the sea. He told the people of the land, "If you need help, just call ‘Bố ơi!’" (Bố means Dad.)
  2. A giant fish spirit made huge waves. "Bố ơi, come and help us!" He tossed a hot stone into its mouth — "Hot, hot, hot!" — and it swam far, far away.
  3. His great wave washed a nine-tailed fox's hill into a lake, and his dragon roar — "RRROAR!" — sent a grumpy tree spirit stomping off. Then he taught the people to plant rice and cook it in bamboo tubes, and they built houses up on posts.
  4. Âu Cơ, a fairy of the mountains, came down to see the flowers. They met, talked and talked, and got married with drums — "Tùng, tùng, tùng!"
  5. Âu Cơ had a bọc, a soft bundle. They waited one, two, three … seven days, it opened, and out came a hundred eggs — "Crack! Crack!" — and a hundred children.
  6. The dragon missed the sea, so they shared: fifty children to the sea with him, fifty to the mountains with her — "Mountain or sea — we'll help each other!" The eldest became the first Hùng King of Văn Lang, and that's why we are con Rồng cháu Tiên.

After the story · car-friendly wondering

"Lạc Long Quân loved the sea and Âu Cơ loved the mountains. Which would you pick — and why?"

"They promised to help each other whenever someone called. Who do you call when you need help — and who do you help?"

"A hundred brothers and sisters! What would be fun about that? What would be tricky?"

Where this story comes from

This is the legend many Vietnamese families tell about where the Vietnamese people come from: Con Rồng cháu Tiên, "children of the Dragon, grandchildren of the Fairy". It is a legend, not history. Its traditional dates are far older than anything archaeology can confirm.

The oldest written telling we know of is "Hồng Bàng thị truyện" (the story of the Hồng Bàng clan), which opens Lĩnh Nam chích quái, a collection of wonder tales in Classical Chinese. It is traditionally credited to Trần Thế Pháp, said to have lived under the late Trần dynasty (14th century), but that can't be proven. Vũ Quỳnh (preface dated 1492) and Kiều Phú (postface dated 1493) separately revised it, and the surviving copies are later still. A shorter version opens Ngô Sĩ Liên's Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư (1479). We read Lĩnh Nam chích quái in Eric Henry's English translation (Rising Asia Journal, "Plucked-up Tales", 2026). We also followed the version long printed in the Grade 6 textbook (Ngữ văn 6), credited "Theo Nguyễn Đổng Chi".

From the old tale: Lạc Long Quân is the son of Kinh Dương Vương and a dragon princess of Lake Động Đình. He teaches people how to dress and how to eat, then goes home to his water palace. When people are in trouble they call, "Father, why don't you come and save us?" and he appears at once; the old text says this is where the word bố, "father", comes from. Âu Cơ gives birth to a sack-like bundle; after seven days a hundred eggs come out, each hatches a baby, and the children grow strong without needing milk. He takes fifty children to the sea, she takes fifty to the mountains, and the eldest becomes Hùng Vương, king of Văn Lang. The tale ends by saying people grew sticky rice, cooked it in bamboo tubes and lived on platforms in the trees, safe from tigers. The school version says he taught people "to farm, raise animals and how to live", so we let him teach the rice and the cooking, and the people build their houses up high themselves. The low grass huts on the early pages echo the "huts woven from grass" in the fox tale.

The parting speech (pages 33–35) closely follows the well-known lines of the school version, in our own translation: "I am of the dragons, from the deep waters; you are of the fairies, from the high mountains…", fifty children to the sea and fifty to the mountains, and "Mountain people and sea people, help each other whenever there is need." The refrain "Mountain or sea — we'll help each other!" shortens that promise, which is in both the old text and the school version.

The three spirits are separate tales in Lĩnh Nam chích quái, and the old versions are not gentle. In "Ngư Tinh" a man-eating fish is tricked into swallowing red-hot iron and killed. Henry's headnote credits Lạc Long Quân; his base manuscript names the hero Đào Thương Long, and he notes that one copy reads Lạc Long Quân. In "Hồ Tinh" a thousand-year-old nine-tailed fox lures people into its cave, and Long Quân sends water to smash its stone hill, leaving the hollow that is now Hồ Tây, the West Lake in Hà Nội. In "Mộc Tinh" the tree spirit demands human sacrifices and is finally destroyed by a magician centuries later, not by Lạc Long Quân. The school version gathers all three under him, and we followed it.

What we changed for a 4-year-old: the spirits are sent away, not killed, and the fox steals fish, not people. We left out Đế Lai (in Lĩnh Nam chích quái Âu Cơ is his consort; in the Toàn thư, his daughter) and had her come down to see the flowers, as in the school version. We left out the bundle being thought an ill omen and put out in a field. The old texts say a hundred sons; we say children. In the old tale Âu Cơ is stranded and pleads with him; we kept his reason and the promise and made the goodbye kind. Apart from the parting speech, the dialogue is our own wording. The repeated "Bố ơi, come and help us!" grows out of the people's call in the old text. Ours: the dragon roar, the wedding drums, the seven little suns, the hundred breakfasts, "And whenever they missed each other, they called — and they came", and the last page for Kai.

Đồng bào (同胞) is an old Sino-Vietnamese word, shared with Chinese, that literally means "same womb". Linking it to Âu Cơ's bọc is a much-loved Vietnamese explanation, not the word's proven origin.

The pictures borrow from the Đông Sơn culture of northern Vietnam, known from bronze drums over two thousand years old such as the Ngọc Lũ drum: a many-pointed star, feather headdresses, stilt houses with saddle roofs, long boats with bird-head prows, and long-billed birds like egrets. That culture is real archaeology but far younger than the legend's dates, so it is a respectful nod, not a claim about what anyone in the legend wore. The dragon's colours and both headdresses are our own picture-book choices. Vietnamese families tell this story in many ways, and grandparents' versions count too.
